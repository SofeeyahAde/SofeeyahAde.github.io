Fintech Credit Risk and Underwriting Optimization

MySQL and Power BI analysis of 39,717 historical LendingClub loans to find where default risk concentrates and what a stricter underwriting rule would cost.

View repository

Business problem

Digital lenders must balance loan growth against credit risk. I set out to answer five questions that support underwriting decisions:

  1. Do larger loans show higher observed default rates?
  2. How does housing status relate to risk and exposure?
  3. How much loan amount is associated with Charged Off records?
  4. How do default rates differ by verification status?
  5. What is the net historical trade-off, loss avoided against profit forgone, of restricting renters who request $20,000 or more?

Approach

I profiled the data in MySQL first (record counts, duplicate IDs, missing values, categories), then grouped loans into tiers: Small up to $10,000, Medium up to $20,000 and High above that. Default rate is Charged Off loans divided by completed loans (Charged Off plus Fully Paid), so Current loans are excluded. For the underwriting scenario I measured net outcome, meaning total payments received minus the amount lent, for the Charged Off and Fully Paid loans in the segment. Each question was answered with a documented query, and Charged Off was treated as the default outcome.

Key findings

Default rises with loan size

13.27% for small loans, 15.01% for medium and 20.25% for high

Housing shows different risk and exposure

Mortgage borrowers have the lowest default rate (13.67%) but more than $224M of exposure. Owners default at 14.89% and renters at 15.36%, with a large share of volume. The OTHER group is highest at 18.37%.

About $68.11M is tied to charged-off loans

Roughly 15.3% of the total loan amount. This is not the same as final accounting loss, since recoveries and fees are not included.

Verified loans defaulted more, not less

16.80% for verified loans, 14.82% for source verified and 12.83% for not verified. This is an association, possibly caused by verification being applied to riskier applicants.

A blanket ban on renters borrowing $20K+ would have cost money

This segment is riskier: its historical default rate is 20.33% on completed loans, against 14.6% for the whole portfolio (401 charged off and 1,571 fully paid, with 123 current loans excluded). But the profit on its repaid loans was larger than the losses on its defaulted ones. A ban would have avoided about $4.37M in losses and given up about $7.05M in profit, a net cost of about $2.68M.

Recommendations

  • Apply stronger risk controls to larger loan requests.
  • Don’t apply a blanket restriction to renters requesting $20K+; it would have cost money. Test risk-based pricing or tighter criteria for this segment instead, and measure the results.
  • Investigate why verified loans default more often.
  • Monitor both default rate and financial exposure, because they measure different risks.

Limitations

  • Historical, observational data: it shows associations, not causes.
  • Charged Off is a proxy for default, and the charged-off amount is not final accounting loss.
  • The underwriting scenario is a historical simulation, not a predictive model.
  • Current loans are excluded from default rates and from the net-outcome calculation because their outcome is not yet known.
  • The underwriting scenario is a look-back on loans that were already approved. It cannot show how rejected applicants would have behaved, and it ignores the time value of money and the lender’s cost of funds.
