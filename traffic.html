Traffic Accident Analysis

Business problem

Which weather, lighting and road conditions carry the highest injury risk per crash, and where should a city transport agency focus safety measures first? Raw injury counts can mislead, because most driving happens in clear weather, daylight and on dry roads, so those conditions dominate the totals. I therefore compared injury rates per crash, and only ranked conditions with at least 1,000 crashes.

Data preparation

In MySQL I inspected the table, removed 31 duplicate records (209,306 down to 209,275), reviewed unknown values in key fields and prepared the date fields. The data covers 2013 to 2025. Injury rate is injuries per 100 crashes, and incapacitating rate is incapacitating injuries per 1,000 crashes. The cleaning and analysis scripts are in the repository.

(https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/oktayrdeki/traffic-accidents)

Questions

Main questions

  1. Which weather condition has the highest injury rate per 100 crashes?
  2. Which lighting condition has the highest incapacitating rate per 1,000 crashes?
  3. How do injury rates differ by road surface?
  4. What share of dry-road crashes have no determined cause?

Supporting questions (counts)

  1. Which weather condition has the highest total injuries?
  2. Which lighting condition has the highest and lowest incapacitating injuries?
  3. Which road surface has the most crashes, and what is its most common cause?
  4. What is the most common first crash type, and when does the most common crash type peak and dip?

Key findings

Lighted darkness has the highest incapacitating rate

Darkness on a lighted road records 49.3 incapacitating injuries per 1,000 crashes, against 34.0 in daylight, roughly 45% higher. Darkness without lighting (45.3) and dawn (43.8) are also above daylight. Daylight has the most incapacitating injuries (4,557) only because about 64% of crashes happen in daylight. Darkness on a lighted road is slightly higher than on an unlit road, and I have not tested why.

Clear weather has the most injuries, but rain has the highest rate

Clear weather accounts for about 78.7% of crashes and 80.3% of injuries. Per 100 crashes, rain is highest among conditions with at least 1,000 crashes at 41.2, against 39.0 for clear weather and 38.3 overall. The gap is modest, so rain is a signal to investigate, not proof of a large effect. Snow is lower than clear weather (30.8), so severe-looking conditions do not automatically mean more injuries per crash.

Road surface makes little difference

Dry roads record 39.6 injuries per 100 crashes and wet roads 41.3. Wet roads have a lower incapacitating rate than dry roads (37.7 against 40.5 per 1,000 crashes). Snowy and icy roads show lower rates per crash, and I have not tested why.

A quarter of dry-road crashes have no determined cause

Dry roads have the most crashes, and 41,610 of them (26.7%) are recorded as Unable to determine. That is a data-quality gap, not a cause. Categories recorded as Unknown also show much lower injury rates, which may reflect incomplete recording and not safer conditions.

Supporting observations

Turning is the most common first crash type: about 64,150 crashes, or 30.7% of the total. The most common crash type, No injury or drive away, peaks in October (11,136) and dips in April (8,463). Month length and the years covered affect these counts, and 2025 is a partial year.

Recommendations

  1. Prioritise low-light conditions, especially darkness on lighted roads, where the incapacitating rate per crash is roughly 30% to 45% above daylight.
  2. Treat rain as a secondary focus, since its gap over clear weather is small.
  3. Improve how crash causes are recorded, and check whether Unknown categories reflect missing injury data.
  4. Investigate turning crashes, because Turning is the most common first crash type.

Limitations

  • Observational data: it shows associations, not causes.
  • No traffic-volume figures, so rates per vehicle or per mile cannot be calculated. The rates here are per crash.
  • 2013 and 2025 are partial years, so annual and monthly comparisons may be affected.
  • Only conditions with at least 1,000 crashes are ranked. Unknown categories and small categories should not be over-interpreted.
