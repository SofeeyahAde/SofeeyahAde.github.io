Healthcare Admissions, Billing and Length of Stay

Business problem

Raw admission records do not show patterns in admissions, billing, hospital activity or length of stay. I turned a healthcare dataset into an interactive report to answer five questions:

  1. What are the overall patterns in admissions, billing, length of stay and demographics over time?
  2. Which demographic groups and conditions account for the largest share of admissions?
  3. How do hospitals and admission types differ in volume, stay and billing?
  4. How does billing vary across conditions, admission types, hospitals and lengths of stay?
  5. What are the most significant patterns in the data?

Data preparation

The Kaggle dataset had 55,500 records. In Power Query I corrected data types, standardised text, converted dates, removed duplicates, created age groups, calculated length of stay and excluded negative billing amounts. That left 49,904 records, and every decision is recorded in a separate cleaning log.

Key findings

Admissions are spread almost evenly across adult age groups

Each group from 20 to 79 accounts for 14.5% to 15.1% of admissions. The 50 to 59 group leads by less than one point, so age alone does not explain admission volume.

Admissions are split evenly by gender

Female patients account for 50.01% and male patients 49.99%.

Admissions were stable from 2020 to 2023

They stayed between 9.86K and 10.14K a year. The lower counts in 2019 (6.62K) and 2024 (3.45K) are consistent with partial coverage, since the data runs from May 2019 to May 2024.

Billing is nearly uniform

Average billing ranges only from $25.28K (cancer) to $25.84K (obesity). Elective ($25.67K) and emergency ($25.57K) admissions are almost identical, so none of these is a meaningful cost driver.

Hospital rankings are not reliable

There are about 39,800 distinct hospital names across 49,904 admissions, so most hospitals have one or two records. The top hospital by billing mainly reflects having more admissions.

Limitations

  • Single-table dataset that does not represent a complete clinical or financial system, and it appears synthetic.
  • No encounter ID, so duplicates were identified from the available encounter fields.
  • Negative billing amounts were excluded because the source did not say whether they were refunds or errors.
  • Findings should not be generalised to all healthcare organisations.
